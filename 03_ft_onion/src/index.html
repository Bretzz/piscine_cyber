How Tor Works: Onion Routing

Tor achieves anonymity by wrapping network packets in multiple layers of encryption (like an onion). Data is routed through three randomly selected voluntary relays across the globe.

Key Principles

  • Guard Node: Knows who you are (your IP), but not what you are accessing or requesting.
  • Middle Node: Knows the IP of the Guard node and the Exit node, but knows neither the client IP nor the final destination.
  • Exit Node: Decrypts the final layer and contacts the destination server. It knows the payload/destination, but has no idea who sent it.

History of Tor

Tor was not created by hackers or cybercriminals; its origins stem from military-grade privacy research aimed at protecting government intelligence communications.

1990s — The US Navy Research Lab

Employees Paul Syverson, Michael G. Reed, and David Goldschlag at the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory (NRL) developed Onion Routing to safeguard U.S. intelligence communications online.

2002 — The Birth of Tor

Computer scientists Roger Dingledine and Nick Mathewson joined Syverson to launch the Freehaven and MIT alpha project called TOR (The Onion Routing project).

2004 — Open Source & EFF Funding

The Naval Research Lab released the code under a free software license. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) funded continued development to promote digital rights and global free speech.

2006 — The Tor Project Founded

The Tor Project, Inc., a 501(c)(3) research-educational nonprofit, was officially founded to maintain and maintain Tor development worldwide.

Pros & Cons of Tor

Pros (Advantages)

  • High Anonymity: Eliminates single points of failure. No single node knows both your IP address and your destination.
  • Censorship Resistance: Bypasses national firewalls and deep packet inspection (DPI) through Tor Bridges and Pluggable Transports.
  • Free & Open Source: Maintained by a non-profit and driven by thousands of global volunteers operating relays.
  • End-to-End Hidden Services: Tor-to-Tor traffic (using .onion addresses) never leaves the network, providing full cryptographic confidentiality.

Cons (Disadvantages)

  • Higher Latency & Low Speed: Routing traffic through three encrypted hops across the world significantly reduces throughput compared to standard web browsing.
  • Malicious Exit Nodes: If reaching an unencrypted HTTP site, an exit node operator can inspect or modify unencrypted payload data.
  • Blocklists: Many mainstream websites, banks, and Cloudflare-protected targets block known Tor exit node IP addresses.
  • Susceptible to Sybil / Traffic Analysis Attacks: An adversary controlling both the entry and exit relays can correlate packet timing to deanonymize users.

How to Host a Tor Hidden Service (.onion)

Tor allows you to host web applications locally and expose them as .onion addresses without needing a public IPv4/IPv6 address, port forwarding, or traditional DNS.

Step 1: Run your Local Application

Ensure your web application (e.g., NGINX, Apache, Express, Go) is listening locally on 127.0.0.1:8080 (or port 80).

Step 2: Configure torrc

Open your system's Tor configuration file (typically /etc/tor/torrc) and add a Hidden Service entry block:

# Hidden Service Configuration
HiddenServiceDir /var/lib/tor/my_onion_service/
HiddenServicePort 80 127.0.0.1:8080

Step 3: Restart Tor & Extract Public Address

Restart the Tor daemon to generate the cryptographic keypair and the address:

sudo systemctl restart tor

Read your generated 56-character v3 .onion domain name:

sudo cat /var/lib/tor/my_onion_service/hostname

Under the Hood: Rendezvous Points

When you host a service, your daemon selects Introduction Points inside the Tor network and publishes its public key to a Distributed Hash Table (DHT). Clients negotiate connections via a middle Rendezvous Point, allowing client and server to communicate without either side discovering the other's IP address.

How to Reach a Tor Service

Connecting to a .onion service requires software capable of routing SOCKS5 traffic through the Tor network.

1. The Easy Way: Tor Browser

The Tor Browser (a hardened fork of Mozilla Firefox) includes the Tor daemon built-in. It handles SOCKS5 proxying, DNS resolution over Tor, and prevents browser fingerprinting out of the box.

2. Programmatic Access / CLI

If reaching an onion service via CLI tools like curl or SSH, route requests through a local Tor daemon SOCKS5 proxy (default port 9050 or 9050/9150):

# Route a curl request over local Tor SOCKS5 proxy
curl --socks5-hostname 127.0.0.1:9050 http://xxxxxx...xxxx.onion

# SSH through a Tor proxy using netcat (nc)
ssh -o ProxyCommand="nc -X 5 -x 127.0.0.1:9050 %h %p" user@xxxx...xxxx.onion

3. How Client Resolution Works

  1. The client requests descriptor details for the target .onion address from Tor's Distributed Hash Table (DHT).
  2. The client selects a random relay to serve as a Rendezvous Point and creates a circuit to it.
  3. The client sends a secret one-time token to one of the service's Introduction Points.
  4. The hidden service receives the message, builds a circuit to the client's chosen Rendezvous Point, presents the token, and establishes an encrypted end-to-end tunnel.
